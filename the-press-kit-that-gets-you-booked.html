The Press Kit That Gets You Booked: What Journalists Actually Want to See

Most press kits are ignored within 8 seconds. Here's what separates the ones that book features from the ones that go straight to trash.

Every week, journalists, podcast producers, and TV bookers receive hundreds of pitches. Most are deleted without a response. A small number get forwarded to an editor with the words: "This one is interesting."

The difference almost always comes down to one thing: the press kit.

Not the pitch. Not the follow-up. The press kit that was — or wasn't — attached.

73% of journalists say they visit a source's press page or media kit before deciding whether to pursue a story. Yet fewer than 1 in 5 small business owners have one that's ready to send.

This guide covers exactly what to include, what to cut, and how to structure a press kit that makes journalists feel like their job just got easier.

Why Most Press Kits Fail

The most common mistake is building a press kit for yourself instead of for the journalist.

Business owners fill press kits with awards, company timelines, product descriptions, and founder bios that read like LinkedIn summaries. Journalists don't want your history. They want to know one thing: what story can I tell about you, and will my audience care?

A press kit that doesn't answer that question within the first 30 seconds is a press kit that doesn't get used.

"The best press kit I ever received had five things on one page. I booked that guest within an hour." — Podcast producer with 400K+ monthly listeners

The 7 Elements of a High-Converting Press Kit

1. The One-Paragraph Hook

Lead with a single paragraph that frames your story from the audience's perspective. Not what you do — what problem you solve, what pattern you've broken, or what result you've created that's surprising or counterintuitive.

Weak: "Anna Tran is a PR strategist and founder of Level Up With Anna with 10 years of experience helping businesses grow."

Strong: "Anna Tran has helped 200+ entrepreneurs get featured in national media without a publicist or a PR budget — using a system that turns a business owner's everyday expertise into press-worthy stories journalists actually want to cover."

The second version creates a story. Journalists trade in stories.

2. Three to Five Suggested Topics or Angles

This is the single most underused element in press kits — and the most valuable to a journalist under deadline pressure.

Give them three to five ready-to-run story angles. Keep each one to two sentences. Make them specific, timely, and tied to something the audience would want to read or hear.

Example angles for a PR strategist:

  • "Why 2026 is the best year in history for small businesses to get press coverage — and how to take advantage of it"
  • "The 15-minute morning routine that keeps entrepreneurs top-of-mind with journalists"
  • "The press kit mistake that's getting business owners ignored (and the fix that takes 20 minutes)"

When you hand a journalist a story angle on a silver platter, you've eliminated the hardest part of their job. That makes you a source they come back to.

3. A Professional Headshot — Not a Selfie

This sounds obvious. And yet.

Your headshot is the first thing that signals whether you're a credible, professional source or someone who wandered in from social media. A clean, high-resolution headshot on a simple background takes an hour and pays dividends for years.

Include at least two versions: portrait (for print and features) and square (for podcasts and social tagging). Make them available as direct download links — not behind a Google Drive login.

4. Previous Media Mentions (Even Small Ones)

Journalists and producers use previous coverage to validate that you're a credible source. They're not looking for the New York Times. They're looking for evidence that someone else already trusted you enough to feature you.

Social proof works on journalists too. A source with three local media features converts at nearly 4x the rate of a source with no prior coverage — even if the outlets are small.

If you don't have any press yet, include speaking appearances, podcast episodes, or even LinkedIn articles that generated significant engagement. The goal is to show a pattern of recognized expertise.

5. Key Stats or Proof Points

Numbers make stories concrete. Include three to five data points that are specific to your results or experience:

  • Number of clients served or results achieved
  • Revenue, growth percentages, or transformations
  • Years of experience in a specific context
  • Any proprietary research or survey data you hold

Journalists love to quote statistics. If yours are compelling and easy to find, they become the backbone of a feature story.

6. Sample Questions for Interviewers

Podcast hosts especially love this. They're often researching multiple guests simultaneously and have limited prep time. A list of eight to ten interview questions tells them you've done the homework — and gives them a roadmap to a great episode.

Write the questions you'd want to be asked. The ones that let you share your best insights, tell your best stories, and deliver the most value to listeners.

7. A Clear, Fast Contact Path

Make it impossible to not reach you. Name, email, phone number, and a booking link if you use one. Don't make a journalist or producer fill out a contact form. They won't. They'll move on to the next source who made it easier.

Format: One Page or One URL

The best press kits are either a single, well-designed PDF or a dedicated press page on your website. Not both — journalists don't want to decide which one to use.

If you go with a web page, make it clean and fast-loading. If you go PDF, keep it to two pages maximum and test it on mobile before sending. A press kit that requires zooming and scrolling is a press kit that doesn't get read.

The Most Important Thing

A press kit doesn't get you press. It removes the friction that was stopping you from getting press you deserved anyway.

The story was always there. The expertise was always real. The press kit just makes it easy for the right person to say yes.

"I've seen $10M businesses get ignored by media because their press materials looked amateur. And I've seen first-year founders land national features because their press kit made the journalist's job effortless." — PR veteran, 18 years in earned media

Most business owners are one good press kit away from their first feature. The ones who build it right don't wait for press — press comes to them.
